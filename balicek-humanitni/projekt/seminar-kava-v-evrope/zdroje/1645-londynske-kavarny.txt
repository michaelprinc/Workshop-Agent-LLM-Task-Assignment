A short account of the coffee houses in London (illustrative text, not a real source)

It is reported that the first house in London where coffee was sold to the
public was opened in the year 1645 by a Greek sailor who had learned the
trade in Constantinople. The price of a cup was one penny, and the drink was
served black, without sugar or milk.

Those who kept these houses were mostly foreigners — Greeks, Turks, and
Lebanese men — and the coffee houses quickly became places where merchants,
writers, and gentlemen gathered after their work. It was said that a man
could pay one penny for a cup of coffee and buy, together with it, "news,
conversation, and company" until nightfall.

The drink was not without its critics. Some physicians wrote against it,
calling it a hot, bitter water that disturbed the stomach and kept men awake
past their natural hours. Others defended it as a sober alternative to the
ale houses, where too much wine was drunk.

By the second half of the century there were, according to one traveller's
count, more than three thousand coffee houses in London alone, though this
number is doubted by later historians.
